## What is Glagolitic?

Glagolitic (Глаголица) is the **oldest known Slavic alphabet**, invented in **863 AD** by the Byzantine brothers **Saint Cyril and Saint Methodius** (Константин-Философ and Михаил-Мефодий) to translate Christian liturgical texts into Old Church Slavonic.

The name comes from the Slavic word *глаголъ* — meaning "word" or "speech."

### Why was it created?

In 863 AD, the Great Moravian Prince Rastislav asked the Byzantine Emperor to send missionaries who could preach in the Slavic language, not Latin or Greek. Cyril and Methodius accepted this mission and created a writing system specifically designed for Slavic sounds — something the Greek and Latin alphabets couldn't fully represent.

Glagolitic had **28–39 letters** (depending on the period and region), each designed to capture the phonetics of Old Church Slavonic with precision.

### What made it unique?

- **Completely original design** — no letters were borrowed from any existing alphabet
- **Ornamental, geometric shapes** — symbols resemble crosses, spirals, and abstract figures
- **Every Slavic sound had its own letter** — including sounds like ж, ш, ч, which have no Latin equivalent
- **Beautiful and mysterious** — it looks more like a medieval cipher than a practical alphabet

## Where was it used?

After the creation of Glagolitic, it spread across several Slavic regions:

- **Great Moravia** (modern Czech Republic, Slovakia) — the birthplace
- **Bulgaria** — adopted under Tsar Boris I, but later replaced by Cyrillic
- **Croatia** — the **last stronghold** of Glagolitic, used in liturgical texts until the **19th century**
- **Serbia, Bohemia, and parts of Russia** — used for a time before Cyrillic took over

The most famous surviving monument is the **Baška Tablet** (Башканский камень, ~1100 AD) — a stone slab with a Glagolitic inscription, now displayed in the Croatian Parliament building.

## Why did it disappear?

Despite its beauty and phonetic precision, Glagolitic was gradually replaced by the **Cyrillic alphabet** (Кириллица). Here's why:

### 1. Cyrillic was easier to learn
Cyrillic was created by the disciples of Cyril and Methodius after they were expelled from Great Moravia. It was based on the **Greek alphabet**, which was already familiar to scholars and clergy. Glagolitic's unique ornamental shapes were harder to learn and write quickly.

### 2. Political pressure
In **Bulgaria** and **Serbia**, rulers promoted Cyrillic as the official script. Glagolitic was associated with older, less powerful traditions. By the **12th century**, Cyrillic had become the standard for Slavic Orthodox Christianity.

### 3. The printing press favored Cyrillic
When printing arrived in Slavic lands, printers chose Cyrillic — it was simpler to carve into metal type. Glagolitic's complex curves were a nightmare for early typesetters.

### 4. Croatia held on the longest
In **Croatia**, Glagolitic survived the longest — protected by Catholic tradition and local pride. Croatian priests continued using it in church services. The Vatican officially recognized Glagolitic liturgy. But even there, by the **mid-19th century**, Latin script had won for secular use, and Cyrillic for Orthodox use. Glagolitic retreated to a handful of parishes.

### 5. The final blow
After **World War II**, the communist government of Yugoslavia suppressed religious traditions. The last regular Glagolitic mass was celebrated in the village of **Učka** in the **1970s**. Today, it survives only as a **cultural symbol** — in decorations, tattoos, and occasional liturgical performances.

## What does this project do?

This is a **single-page web app** (no server, no dependencies) that:

- Translates Russian text (Cyrillic) → Glagolitic in real time
- Translates English text (Latin) → Glagolitic in real time
- Includes a reference chart of all Glagolitic letters
- Lets you copy the result to clipboard

Just open `index.html` in your browser and start typing.
